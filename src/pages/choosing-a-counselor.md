If you've found our articles useful and you're serious about moving your career forward, there's a good chance you'd benefit from working with someone one-on-one — to sort through your options and build a realistic plan. Here's how to find the right person.

## Do it yourself, or get help?

Plenty of career progress happens on your own. But a good counselor or coach can save you months of spinning, help you see blind spots, and keep you accountable. Our [team page](/bios) is a good starting point: each of us lists our background, specialties, and how to reach us, so you can compare and contrast in one place.

## Counselor or coach?

This is the first distinction you'll notice in the field, and it's worth understanding:

- **Counselors** typically hold a master's degree in a counseling specialty and may be licensed (LPC). Beyond assessing your strengths and teaching sound job-search approaches, they're trained to help with the personal issues that affect a career — anxiety, confidence, depression, identity, big life transitions.
- **Coaches** tend to focus more on the here and now: partnering with you on the job search, clarifying goals, and building strategies to reach your professional potential.

There's real overlap, and many excellent practitioners blend both. Knowing which emphasis you want helps you narrow the field.

## Finding a good fit

1. **Review several bios** and pick a few people who resonate with you.
2. **Read what they've written.** Our [articles](/blog) are a window into how each of us thinks. That usually narrows things to two to four people worth contacting.
3. **Widen the net if you need to** — ask friends for referrals, or search the web for phrases like *"career counselor Portland"* or *"career coach"* plus your specialty.

## Test-driving a counselor

Once you have a short list of two or three, do a little research (their website, LinkedIn, listings on sites like Psychology Today or Yelp), then reach out by email or phone. Most of us respond promptly and offer a brief complimentary consultation — a chance for both of you to see whether it's a good match.

A few broadly useful questions to ask:

- **Approach** — How would you help someone like me choose, change, or advance my career?
- **Qualifications** — How long have you practiced? What's your education and training? What do you specialize in?
- **Cost** — What's your fee? Roughly how many sessions? Do you accept insurance or EAP?
- **Your gut** — No set question here. Notice the person's warmth, insight, and style. Did you feel understood and helped, even in that first conversation?

After your consultations, rate each person 1–5 on those points. The highest total — along with your instinct — usually points to the right choice.

## Going further

If you don't click with anyone on our team, ask — most of us are happy to recommend a colleague who specializes in your situation, whether that's a particular industry, an emotional concern, or a specific history like long-term unemployment or returning to work after time away. And if you're outside the Portland area, several of us offer counseling by phone or video.

## The upside

Yes, this is more effort than picking someone to do your taxes. But notice how far you've already come — from *wishing* for a new job to *committing* to your career development with a qualified professional of your choosing. A skilled counselor can help you understand your interests, personality, skills, and values, find a genuine career fit, and shorten your search. That's well worth the investment.
