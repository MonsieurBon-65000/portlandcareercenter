![void blog (002)](/images/void-blog-002.jpg)It’s the time in your career transition between “getting clear about your direction” and “landing a job.” _The void_ is when you’re typically sending out resumes, applying for jobs, trying to land interviews by connecting with your network, or interviewing—all without landing a job offer. It’s when you’re trying to get noticed but no one’s paying attention.

It’s like standing by the side of the busy street, on your toes, arm extended, waving to every car that looks like a taxi and being passed by, every single time. The first few times it happens, you chalk it up to bad luck. As the sixth taxi passes without stopping, you amp it up, frantically waving both arms, shouting “You hoo, over here!” After you lose count of the taxis that have gone by or how long you’ve been standing there, you’re miffed and discouraged. You give up and wonder “what in the world is going on here?” Should you have worn a different color coat? What if you were a few inches taller or had a louder voice?

**Symptoms of the job search void**. What makes this void so frustrating is the lack of feedback. Like trying to flag down a taxi, or get noticed by a prospective employer, we modify our behavior based on the feedback we get. It’s how we gauge whether we’re on track or not and operating without it is awkward. The typical hiring process just doesn’t operate with a lot feedback built into it (it should and hopefully this will change). And that’s what makes it feel so uncomfortable and unsettling.

In almost everything else that we do in our day-to-day lives there’s a feedback component to tell us how we’re doing: From the guy behind you who honks when the light turns from red to green, to the treadmill that shows you how many calories you’ve burned, to the pet that greets you warmly when you come home (or not, if you have a cat). We’re hard-wired as humans to receive this kind of feedback. It’s part of our social construct and we use it to help guide us.

When you’re in this void, you feel lost, unsettled, and unsure of yourself. There’s no one revealing what keywords your resume is missing and why you didn’t get an interview. Even if you ask for feedback, like after an interview, you’re likely to get an answer that sounds like “We decided on a candidate whose experience and background was a better fit.” That’s not helpful at all. You just want an honest answer, a little nugget of truth that will help you understand the unwritten rules of the crazy job search game. It’s especially confusing for anyone who hasn’t looked for a new job in a while.

Shea identified with, and was stuck in, this void. She had been with her last employer for 28 years. She was trying to make a career pivot and was so frustrated that she couldn’t get a single interview, at jobs for which she appeared overqualified. As frustrated as she was, and despite not getting any reinforcing feedback during this process, Shea continued to apply for jobs online as her primary search strategy.

**Time to shift up strategy?**  What made Shea stick to this mainstream approach, even when it wasn’t getting results? She was feeling completely powerless – which is what happens when we’re feeling vulnerable and exposed. Who likes putting themselves out there, repeatedly, without any feedback? (That’s a rhetorical question. Of course you don’t. You’re sane.)  But sending out resumes/applying for jobs online feels less risky than, say, asking a contact to spend a few minutes with you so you can find out about the company. Asking for help takes a little bravery – and a willingness to face rejection or embarrassment.

What can you do if you’re feeling powerless?  Just keep on showing up as the best version of yourself.  Be your quirky, interesting self and stop making up false stories about what may or may not be happening – too old, overqualified, no network, etc. Take a break (see Taking a Break from Job Search), and regroup. Talk to a career counselor, invite a former colleague who you loved working with to join you for coffee, or find someone who’s recently found a new job and find out what worked for them.

Stand on the other side of the street. Be brave and be you. You’ll survive a moment of embarrassment or awkwardness. I promise, and in so doing you will begin to get out of the job search void.
