## Get the Support You Deserve

"When I am sick, family and friends know to bring chicken soup and words of comfort,” my client, Sandy, told me. When her mom died, she said, “people had on hand some words of solace. However, now that I’m among the long-term unemployed, they don’t know how to offer comfort.”

In fact, some people who are unemployed just avoid social contact because they’ve experienced people being downright insensitive. Yet we all need support during times of turmoil and distress. I believe that the “help” given by friends and family comes in three categories: toxic, teachable, and supportive. Our goal should be to maximize contact with those who are a valuable resource to us and minimize contact with those whose attitudes pose a barrier to our progress.

## Toxic

Even people who care about you deeply can be toxic to your highest career goal. Mike S., in his thirties, found his father toxic to his career independence since he was invested in having his only son join him in his construction business. When his dad started lecturing him on the topic, Mike would become anxious and raise his voice which sometimes led to a fight. After these conversations Mike found himself procrastinating on his next career transition tasks. First, I helped him practice techniques to avoid being triggered by his dad by calmly asserting his own preferences. Second, I helped him understand that he had to turn off the part of himself that had internalized his father’s sentiments. He learned to notice and reject the inner voice that said his career choice needed to make his father happy. When his own feet were clearly committed to his path as a green entrepreneur, he could resume more frequent conversations with his dad—staying on safer topics like sports.

## Teachable

Teachable people are of genuine goodwill, but need some coaching to understand how to be really helpful to you. You can educate these eager but misguided supporters so that they can help you in ways that you’ll truly appreciate. For example, you might need a listening ear during self-assessment, a baby sitter for networking events, or a cheerleader prior to an interview.

A useful coaching technique is to praise an eager supporter for their positive intent, and then model what you need. “Honey, I love it when you tell me that I am smart and can do anything I set my mind to. It would also be helpful if you would tell me about the specific skills that I use when I am being ’smart.’ Is it my intuition on how to handle that cranky neighbor? See if you can ’catch me being smart’ and describe what you see. I’m too close to me to see myself accurately.”

## Supportive

Supportive people have a knack for giving you accurate, positive feedback on your strengths, and can find the right words of comfort when you are plagued by doubts. They are interested in learning more about each phase of your career search process and enjoy witnessing your progress. Supportive people know when and how to reassure you on a bad day, perhaps with tea and a walk rather than unsolicited advice.

You may want to focus some attention on bringing new supportive people into your life while going through your search process. One client had a mother who was not very supportive so she arranged a regular call to a friend’s mom to hear a motherly voice say, “You can do it. I know you are so great!”

## Por****tland Job Support Groups

A great resource for building more empathic support into your life is a career transition support group. Two members of our group of career counselors, myself and Anne Bryant, MA, LPC, facilitate career support groups. Anne’s group balances focus on emotional issues and career tactics. It meets weekly on Mondays from 4 to 6 p.m. at her downtown office. My group is specifically for women over 50 seeking employment or contract work. It meets on the second and fourth Monday of each month from 7 to 9 p.m. at 2625 SE Hawthorne.  
Call in advance to attend either group.

“The benefit of joining a group like this,” according to Martha, a participant in my group, “is that other members can support you emotionally, help with practical things you need to do—like providing feedback on cover letters or practicing questions for interviews, and even connect you with people, organizations and events that might be of value to you.”
