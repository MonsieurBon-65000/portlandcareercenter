## Career Transition: Bad or “Iffy” References

If you’ve been fired, asked to leave, or had serious friction with your supervisor, you naturally want to forget that rocky chapter of your life. When you apply for a new job and must provide references, the situation calls for you to revisit the unwanted chapter from the past. In our practice, we meet many wonderful, competent, and vital people who have been fired. So, speaking from the heart, we offer you both strategies and optimism.

If your negative experience was years ago, and you have positive references from more recent and relevant positions, there is no reason to fret. It’s only if your recent experiences were dicey that you need a strategy to off-set potential harm.

## Before the Interview

It is important to learn if your past employer gives references. Most large employers only verify dates of employment and will not comment on performance because they fear potential lawsuits. You can usually call the HR department and ask them their policy on references. If you were fired, find out how they will respond to questions like “Would you rehire this person?” If your past employer does give references and you don’t feel confident in the quality of the reference, try to circumvent or delay a conversation between this individual and your potential employer. If only a cover letter and resume are requested, omit listing references. If it is an application, and you are still employed with that company, note “please do not contact,” and the potential employer will assume that the reason is that you do not want the supervisor to know that you plan to leave. Let them make this assumption, which gives you more time to build a positive relationship through the interview and other references before they could possibly hear the supervisor’s negative perspective. If you are no longer employed at this recent employer, and they ask for the supervisor’s name, you only have two choices and neither of them are ideal: you can include the name or write in the box “prefer to discuss in person.”

## During the Interview

It’s fortunate for you that references are often not asked for and rarely contacted prior to the interview. You can enter the interview assuming that the interviewer’s perspective has not been tainted by a negative reference. What you say in the interview can shape how they will later receive the negative input. For example, Marty was let go from her position as copyeditor by a boss who focused on speed, which was not one of Marty’s strengths. In the interview, Marty was asked to articulate her strengths. “I pride myself on accuracy and a perfect final product that goes out in every printed piece. While I may not be the fastest copywriter, in the long run I am very efficient for the company because of the lack of embarrassing mistakes or the need to reprint materials.” In addition, when asked “Why are you interested in working with us here at Pasta Supreme?” she responded, “I’ve carefully reviewed your website and materials and see that they are error-free. I know that you are well-matched to my steadfast commitment to quality over speed.” As you can imagine, the interviewers were not surprised when her prior supervisor reported on her inability to keep up with the expected pace.”

## Successful Client Strategies

There is not a “one-size-fits-all” solution for “iffy references,” and you’ll need to balance emotional, tactical, and sometimes legal issues in choosing an approach. Below are some options that have been successfully used by clients:  
• Told the prospective employer (truthfully) that the employer policy is not to release references so you have substituted the name of someone from volunteer experience or a colleague.  
• Secured a short-term contract job in their field from a supportive colleague or friend who did not require a reference so that the most recent reference would be from a different individual.  
• Explained that the issues that arose in that position were due to specific skill weaknesses that will not be relevant in the new position.  
• Used the name of an earlier supervisor rather than the most recent one.  
• Used a supportive member of the board or a vendor as a reference in place of the supervisor.  
• Explained to the prospective employer the nature of the conflict and gave the names of three other people in the organization so that they can get a fuller perspective.  
• Directed the prospective employer to a large pool of positive references on LinkedIn to give a range of perspectives.

If you find any of these actions emotionally demanding (re-igniting the anger, sadness, and/or disappointment that you experienced around the unhappy ending), Gail Nicholson’s blog post (http://tinyurl.com/crdmbmu) can help you explore many self-care tools that reduce emotional upheaval.
